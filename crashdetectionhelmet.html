    Crash-Detection Bicycle Helmet

    Developed for ASU's EGR304 Integrated System Design Course

    The Problem: The Integrated Systems Design course tasked students with concepting and construction of a piece of safety equipment for individuals riding street-bikes alongside cars on public roads. This project was selected in conjunction with ASU's push towards a carbon neutral transportation network for commuting students.

  • Bicycle riders are exposed to significant risks when riding alongside automobiles, especially given the growth of vehicle-operator distractions.
  • The vast majority of cyclist-vehicle fatalities arise from a lack of reporting. Often, drivers will not report the impact, resulting in long periods between accident and arrive of emergency services.
  • Minimizing the risk to cyclists will likely boost the percentage of students traveling via bicycle, which will lower the average carbon emissions per mile for students that must travel for class.

    • Design Considerations: Our team considered a few design concepts prior to landing on our final solution. The greatest of which is cost, of which we were given a limited budget provided through the university. We also were given only five months from initial conceptual design to final prototype, which given the PCB design requirements limited the scope of production due to long turnaround time for circuit board manufacturing. Finally, we were required to demo the unit at a showcase, which means the final prototype needed to be mobile as well as function for the full five-hours continuously.

    Initial Design and Prototyping : To maximize our work time, our team elected to start our prototyping phase early. We developed testing criteria to test before we began prototyping.

  • System must be able to discern a crash event from normal riding behavior.
    • Strap the sensor bus to a moving bicycle and ride around to ensure no false-positive
    • Attach sensors to helmet, wrap in a towel, and throw from various heights until the system triggers.
  • System must be able to report crash event
    • Send/Recieve commands between helmet and smartphone
    • Capable of calling phone-numbers and reporting information
  • The system must have a convenient, simple cancellation system.
    • Test cancellation system one-handed, wearing thick gloves, with no visuals.
    • Ensure auditory cue is vivid enough to be notified over loud ambient noise.

    Crash Detection : Crash detection is the main focus of our project, and a large part of that is being able to discern a crash event from normal riding behavior. Our methodology for this was to use an accelerometer alongside the microcontroller to log the position change per CPU cycle. This single sensor is responsible for the majority of the functionality. Crashes have a very unique signature when logged, primarily due to the rapid acceleration spike followed by a longer period of reduced movement. During testing, we logged data coming off the sensor as we wrapped it in increasing dampening material and threw it from increasing heights. We found that even if cushioned by two thick towels, the accelerometer would still log a significant spike in acceleration when falling from a squatting height, which would be less than is required from a normal rider. This is well within our testing parameters, accounting for the potential of someone to break their fall due to a jacket or backpack.

    Final Design: Our final prototype externally consists of exclusively a helmet with a USBC Port on it. All hardware is internally mounted, being resealed by reinserting the internal foam cutaway to minimize potential damage risk from external components, along with protecting the safety equipment.

    Post-Mortem: Our team learned a lot due to the cross-disciplinary project. Everyone had to learn a little about everyone else's field to cooperate, and it certainly helped us all become better engineers. The only major hardware hiccup we ran into was that Windows only allows you to poll a single webcam at a time, and the onboard processor for the robot could not switch camera feeds rapidly enough to handle the motion tracking across two cameras. Our final solution was to offload the HD camera to a second computer that would encode relevant information and send it back to the primary machine for the tracking solution.
